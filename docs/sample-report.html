Synthetic focus group

Oatlight Barista concept test

September 27, 2026 16:59 · anthropic (moderator: claude-sonnet-5, participant: claude-haiku-4-5-20251001, analyst: claude-sonnet-5) · seed 42

Objective: Find out whether weekly milk buyers would switch to a premium oat milk, what would stop them, and which of the product's claims they find believable.

What we heard

Weekly milk buyers found Oatlight Barista's price impossible to justify against an unproven sustainability claim, and group discussion made them more skeptical and less interested, not more.

Across both groups, appeal and purchase intent for Oatlight Barista fell sharply after discussion (appeal mean 3.92→2.08; purchase intent 3.08→1.50), driven mainly by the $5.49 price versus what participants currently pay for dairy or store-brand oat milk. The 14-day freshness claim was the most believed and valued benefit because it was concrete and personally verifiable, while the 'regenerative farming' claim was viewed as unsubstantiated marketing language that most people could not define and would not pay extra for even if proven true. The 'foams like dairy' claim only resonated with participants who make coffee at home daily; those who buy coffee out or pour milk directly into drip coffee were indifferent to it. Most participants converged on a fair price of roughly $4.25–$4.50 assuming freshness savings and farming claims were independently verified, but several stated no amount of proof would close the price gap for them.

Themes

Price premium is the dominant, largely unmovable barriermost

Nearly every participant in both groups anchored on the $5.49 price against their current spend ($2.50–$4 for dairy or store-brand oat milk), and most said the premium wouldn't be justified even with better proof of claims. A few said no evidence would change their answer because dairy or cheaper oat milk already 'works fine.'
“It doesn't land—it's a hard no for me. I'm paying like three-sixty at Costco for the same amount, so I'm not paying fifty percent more when what I've got works fine”Marcus
“I'm already paying $3.99 for dairy at Kroger, so $5.49 is a bigger jump for me than it probably is for some of you—that's more than forty percent”Claire
“A dollar-fifty on top of what I'm already not buying? That's a non-starter for me.”Grace

14-day freshness is the most credible and appealing claimmost

Participants across both groups spontaneously called out the freshness claim as tangible and measurable, something they could verify themselves by tracking waste, in contrast to vaguer marketing language. Some, however, said it solved a problem they don't actually have because they finish milk before it spoils.
“The 14-day freshness is actually useful—that's real, I can measure that.”Owen
“the 14-day freshness is actually useful to me—I hate throwing out milk that goes bad after a week.”Aisha
“I go through my Costco carton in less than two weeks anyway, so that's not solving an actual problem I have.”Marcus

Regenerative farming claim is distrusted without independent verificationmost

Participants overwhelmingly said the phrase 'regenerative farming' was vague marketing language they couldn't define or verify, and most explicitly rejected certification logos or QR codes from the company itself as sufficient proof, wanting independent third-party data instead.
“I honestly don't know what regenerative farming actually *does*—better soil? Less water? I have no idea.”Hannah
“I write this stuff for a living, and there's a huge gap between what sounds good on packaging and what's actually happening in the fields.”Nadia
“I'm not going to trust a QR code or a certification logo I've never heard of—that's just moving the marketing one step over.”Yuki

Foaming claim only matters to daily at-home coffee makersseveral

The 'foams like dairy' claim, positioned as the product's core reason for being, only registered with participants who make espresso-style drinks at home every day; those who buy coffee out or simply pour milk into drip coffee said it was irrelevant to them.
“the foaming thing matters—I actually notice it when I'm making my coffee at home every day, and bad foam bugs me.”Yuki
“No, I don't really notice the foam thing—I just pour it straight in while the coffee's brewing, so it all mixes together anyway.”Claire
“The "froths like dairy" claim? That's testable, so I'm actually less skeptical about that one.”Nadia

Even with proof, sustainability doesn't move price willingness for mostseveral

Several participants explicitly separated 'believability' from 'willingness to pay,' saying that even if the regenerative farming claim were proven true, it would not shift their price threshold because environmental impact is a low personal priority relative to cost.
“Even if they proved it works, I'm not paying $5.49 until the freshness savings actually pencil out in my spreadsheet. The environmental piece is nice, but it's not worth forty percent more to me right now.”Claire
“even if they proved the regenerative farming works, it still wouldn't change my price threshold because I care about environment maybe a three out of ten.”Joy
“I'm not paying extra for environmental stuff, and I'm not gonna pay extra because the oats are somehow better for my coffee either.”Mateo

Where participants split

  • Whether proof of the regenerative farming claim would change buying behavior: Omar and Marcus (Group 1) said they'd pay more if the data were solid, while Claire, Hannah, Joy, and most of Group 2 (Mateo, Marcus, Grace) said proof would not move their price threshold since price/waste concerns dominate.
  • Sensitivity to the foaming claim split along coffee routine: daily at-home coffee makers (Owen, Yuki, Omar in Group 1) valued it, while those who buy coffee out or drink drip coffee (Hannah, Joy, Claire, Grace, Mateo) found it irrelevant.
  • Price sensitivity itself varied: some participants (Nadia, Aisha, Lucia in Group 2) said the $1.50 premium 'doesn't bother' them and performance/proof was the real gate, while most others treated price as the primary, non-negotiable barrier.
  • Fair-price estimates ranged from a hard floor near dairy price ($2.00, Hannah) to willingness to pay full price or more with proof (Lucia said she'd pay $5.49 without hesitation if claims were real; Omar would stretch to $4.75–$5).

Open questions

  • Would an actual in-cup trial (taste, foam performance) change stated purchase intent, since several participants said they need to 'try it' before judging the price-value tradeoff?
  • What specific independent certification or data source would participants trust enough to actually believe the regenerative farming claim, beyond generic references to 'third-party' or 'USDA-style' certification?
  • Why did overall appeal and purchase intent drop so sharply after discussion (versus pre-discussion ratings)—was this driven primarily by price anchoring from peers, or by growing skepticism toward the regenerative claim as the conversation progressed?
  • Whether a lower price point (the ~$4.25–$4.50 range participants converged on) would actually convert stated intent into real purchase behavior, or whether skepticism about claims would still suppress trial even at that price.

Session protocol

Participants read the concept before the session and each rated it privately. The moderator then ran the discussion guide below. When the moderator put a question to the whole group, everyone gave a first reaction before hearing the others. At the end, each participant saw the whole discussion and their own first answer, and rated again in private.

Discussion guide

TopicGuide question
1. Current habitsWalk me through the milk you buy today and how it ends up in your coffee.
2. First reactionHere is Oatlight Barista. What is your honest first reaction?
3. Price and valueIt costs $5.49, about $1.50 more than the leading oat milk. How does that land?
4. BelievabilityThe carton says the oats come from regenerative farms. What does that mean to you, and do you believe it?
